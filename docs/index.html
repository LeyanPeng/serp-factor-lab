SERP Factor Lab Certificate of calibration Ref. SFL‑01  ·  28 Aug 2026

You can’t check a weight against Google.
So I built a Google you can check.

“This one is 0.84, this one is 0.73 — is that accurate? How do we test it?”

Your question stuck with me after we spoke. I sat down to write you a list of ranking factors, and I couldn’t finish it — because I’d be doing the exact thing that bothers me about this industry. I’d be telling you what I think matters, and neither of us would have any way to check whether I was right.

So I came at it backwards. Google will never show us its weights, so no number we produce can ever be checked against the real thing. But it can be checked against a search engine whose weights we set ourselves. If my method can’t find the right answer in a world where I already know the right answer, it has no business being trusted in a world where I don’t.

That’s the whole project. Over the weekend I built a fake Google — it has position bias, a re‑ranking stage, one factor that’s measured badly, and a completely worthless decoy sitting right next to it — then ran a full modelling pipeline at it and made it mark its own work.

What I’m actually after isn’t a better list of factors. It’s a way to put an honest error bar on any list, including the 20–40 you already use. Everything below was printed by one command in 80 seconds — nothing on this page was typed in by hand.

WHY

What this is for, and how you would know it works

You put it precisely on the call: Google does not tell us, so we work by association. The trouble with association is that it says nothing about its own errors. A correlation study hands back a number whether or not that number means anything, and it hands it back to three decimal places. Nothing in the method ever raises its hand.

That is not an academic worry for an agency. A factor table is a budgeting document. It decides whether a quarter of a retainer goes on writer hours, on links, or on Core Web Vitals. If the table is wrong it does not feel wrong — it feels precise. And afterwards nobody can tell, because rankings move for a hundred reasons at once and the counterfactual was never measured. The failure is silent, and it repeats every quarter.

So the question underneath “is 0.84 really bigger than 0.73” is a harder one: what would have to be true for a weight to be worth acting on, and how would we find out? That is what this answers. Not with a better number — with four tests a number has to survive before anyone spends money on it.

The four tests, in order

TestWhenThe questionWhat passing buys
1. Recovery
done — in the repo		 offline On a world whose answers I already know, does the estimator find them? An error bar you can legitimately attach to a live number. This is the only test that can ever be marked, because it is the only one with an answer key.
2. Agreement week 1
live SERPs		 Does it order real search results the way Google orders them? NDCG@10 on queries it has never seen, against an honest baseline. Permission to continue. If it cannot beat plain semantic similarity on its own, the factor model adds nothing and we stop there rather than dress it up.
3. Forecast
the one that matters		 week 5
onward		 Freeze the model. Predict next month's rank movement on a held‑out panel. Then score the prediction against what actually happened. A forecast, plus the calibration curve saying how much of it to believe. Only this test asks the model to predict anything.

reads out ~week 10		 Change one factor on real client URLs, hold a matched control group, and measure the difference. A number you can put in a contract. Nothing else on this list earns that.

Every ranking-factor study I have ever read stops at test 2. It describes today's search results and calls that a model. But a description of today that cannot call tomorrow's movement is not a prediction, and you cannot budget against a description. Test 3 is cheap — it needs no new data, only the discipline to freeze a model and write the prediction down before looking.

What would make me drop the whole approach. If test 3 comes back uncalibrated — predicted rank movement uncorrelated with what actually moved — then factor weights are the wrong instrument for that vertical, and the right thing to do is say so rather than ship it anyway. That is roughly six weeks to a clear failure state. It is deliberate: a method that cannot fail is not a method, and the reason SEO factor tables never die is that none of them was ever built so that it could.

Why this is worth doing at all

The industry sells confidence. Every tool prints a number; none prints an error bar. That holds up until a client asks “you told us this would work — what happened?” and there is no answer, because nothing was measured and rankings move for reasons nobody recorded.

The alternative is not more numbers. It is fewer claims, each carrying what it is worth, what test produced it, and when it was last checked — and a stated rule that anything which has not passed test 3 or test 4 does not reach a client at all. That is a smaller thing to sell and a much more defensible one, and as far as I can tell nobody in this market is selling it.

§ 1

Results

In this harness those two numbers are literally the truth — content depth is set to 0.84 and site authority to 0.73. Here is what two pipelines say about them.

Uncorrected — the standard build
Content depth truth 0.84 0.13
Site authority truth 0.73 0.57
Keyword density truth 0.00 0.33

It reports a clear winner and claims 115 queries were enough. It has the ranking backwards, and it says so with a tight interval. Confidently wrong is worse than unsure.

After three corrections
Content depth truth 0.84 0.65
Site authority truth 0.73 0.56
Keyword density truth 0.00 0.04

It refuses to call it at 1,200 queries and quotes the panel that would settle it: ~36,000. Position‑bias weighting, errors‑in‑variables, and bootstrapping whole SERPs instead of rows.

Then we ran it at 37,000 queries, the size it asked for. Content depth 0.76, site authority 0.59, difference 95% interval [+0.10, +0.24] — separated. The power calculation quoted a price for certainty, we paid it, and we got certainty. That loop is the product.

Where the instrument fails, and by how much

Deviation from the known truth, one row per factor. The correction moves two rows a very long way and leaves the rest almost where they were — which is the point: you cannot tell which two without a harness.

uncorrected corrected truth

Two failures worth more than the eight successes

The invented factor. Keyword density has a true weight of exactly zero and gets 0.33. The cause is not collinearity, it is measurement: content depth is hard to extract cleanly (boilerplate, navigation, pagination) and the decoy is trivial to extract. A regression credits whichever variable carries the cleanest signal, not whichever one causes the outcome. The fix is a re‑measurement audit — hand‑extract 300 pages, compare against the crawler, feed the reliability into the correction. One afternoon, and the highest‑return item in the whole project.

The vanished factor. The click signal reads 0.16 against a truth of 0.59. The usual construction — observed CTR minus average CTR at that position — looks like it removes position bias and does the opposite: position is caused by quality, so subtracting the positional mean subtracts the quality with it. Dividing by an examination probability estimated from a randomised slice recovers most of it (the exponent comes back at 0.99 against a true 1.00). The residual gap is irreducible click noise. We ship the factor with the gap attached.

More data fixes neither. Run the uncorrected pipeline at 1,200 queries and at 15,000 and it returns the same wrong answer with a narrower interval. Bias and variance are different problems and only one of them has a budget solution.

Weights are not global

Intent clusters are discovered from SERP shape with no labels — adjusted Rand index 0.96 against the true grouping. Site authority then carries 3.4× the weight in YMYL finance that it carries in local services (true ratio 4.4×; we understate it, and say so). A link retainer priced flat across a client book is overcharging some of those clients and undercharging the rest.

The uncomfortable one

Ranked by positions gained per unit of effort, the winner is keyword density — the factor whose true weight is zero. It wins because it is cheap to change and correlated with something that works, and both the fixed‑effects and the matching estimator credit it, because both are observational and inherit the measurement problem whole. An agency that ranked its roadmap this way would spend a quarter shipping nothing, and every number on the page would agree with it the entire time.

§ 3

Pointing it at the real Google

Everything above happens in a world I control. The obvious objection is that none of it touches Google, so here is exactly what transfers, what has to be bought, and what runs every week once it is live.

A live estimate arrives as 0.65, and this estimator was off by 0.19 on a factor of this kind when the answer was checkable. That second clause is the whole contribution. No competitor ships it because no competitor has anywhere to measure it.

The one thing that looked impossible

Position bias is corrected here using a 5% slice of SERPs shown in random order — the exponent comes back at 0.99 against a true 1.00. You cannot randomise Google, so on paper that correction does not survive contact with reality.

It does, because Google randomises for you. The same URL sits at position 4 one week and position 9 the next, for the same query, hundreds of times a month across a panel. Comparing that URL's click‑through rate against itself at two different positions identifies the examination curve without anyone intervening — intervention harvesting rather than intervention. For client sites the raw material is already sitting in Search Console: same page, same query, different average position, week by week.

What actually runs, and when

Agile, concretely

“Adjusts quickly” usually means nothing. Here it means one specific thing: every factor carries a live interval, and the weekly refit either keeps it inside that interval or does not. Inside, nothing happens. Outside, that factor is flagged with a date, and the date is the first evidence of a core update — typically before it is announced, because the panel is watching a stable set of queries rather than a client's own traffic.

Nothing about that alarm reaches a client on its own. It opens a question; a split test closes it. The governance rule in § 6 is not decoration — it is what stops a drift alarm from becoming an invoice.

§ 4

The knowledge graph

A search engine does not rank strings, it ranks entities and the relations between them. A model built only from term‑overlap features is describing the wrong object, and no amount of statistical correction repairs a feature that measures the wrong thing.

We cannot see Google's weights. We can see two things Google will hand over: its answer — the ten pages it picked — and its reading of any page, because Cloud Natural Language's analyzeEntities returns the entities Google's own extractor finds along with a salience score for each. Pool the first, run it through the second, and the topic's entity graph is Google's, not ours.

Why this is a knowledge graph and not a word graph

Three properties, and knowledge_graph.py prints evidence of all three on a real nine‑page SERP:

PropertyWhat the run shows
Linking “banking licence”, “full banking licence” and “authorised UK bank” collapse into one node. Surface form is not identity. A co‑occurrence graph over words scores those as three unrelated strings.
Typing nodes carry a type — SCHEME, REGULATION, SOFTWARE, GOV — so “which regulator governs this topic” is a query you can run, not a thing you eyeball.
Relations edges are typed: Banking licence —PROTECTED_BY→ FSCS, not “these two appeared near each other”.

A work order, for a writer

The same graph names what the thin page is missing, ordered by how much the SERP cares:

Arranged overdraft · Cash deposit charge · Monthly account fee · Current Account Switch Service · FSCS · Banking licence · QuickBooks · Xero

That is a content brief, derived from Google's own ten choices rather than from an opinion in a meeting.

And the finding nobody wants but everybody needs. The client brand in that run is absent from the topic graph entirely. When that happens, on‑page work cannot fix it: no amount of rewriting moves a brand Google has not yet accepted as an entity in the topic. That is a schema.org, sameAs, Wikidata and citations problem, on a different budget and a longer timeline — and it is far cheaper to say so in month one than in month nine.

Factors

34 in six groups, sorted by evidence tier rather than by folklore. Every entry carries a measurement method, a source, a marginal cost and a refresh cadence — a factor you cannot measure weekly at a price you can defend is not a factor, it is an opinion.

Tier B  14 factors

Trial record and leak. Pandu Nayak's DOJ testimony confirming click data as a core signal and NavBoost's 13‑month rolling window; the 2024 Content Warehouse schema exposing siteAuthority, titlematchScore, OriginalContentScore and three separate date fields that make faked freshness detectable.

Refused  6 entries

Keyword density, absolute word count, GA bounce rate, Domain Authority as a lever, “LSI keywords”, meta keywords. Listed with reasons, because half of a factor list is the half you leave out.

Six groups: query‑document relevance (6) · site authority (5) · page quality (7) · user‑interaction proxies (5) · technical delivery (6) · SERP competitive context (5). Three that usually go missing and shouldn't:

  • Branded search demand. Cheap, strong, and almost nobody tracks it — the best available proxy for the site‑level trust term.
  • Passage‑level max similarity. Predicts AI Overview citation far better than page‑level similarity does, and citation is now a separate outcome from position.
  • Return visit rate, from client Search Console. Available to an agency and structurally unavailable to a tool vendor, which is exactly why it belongs on the list.
§ 6

Method

Google is a pipeline, not a function

Modelling it as one weight vector is the root error. Four stages, modelled separately: candidate retrieval (a classification problem) → base ranking (learning to rank) → twiddler re‑ranking, much of which is one‑sided demotion rather than symmetric weight → SERP assembly and AI Overview citation, which is a separate binary model because being cited and ranking third are different outcomes.

Two heads, and a rule connecting them

The interpretable head is a pairwise logistic on within‑query differences — differencing inside a query gives query fixed effects for free, so everything about the query itself cancels. Its coefficients are the numbers you are allowed to quote as “0.84”. The performance head is a gradient‑boosted ranker.

The rule: if the boosted head beats the linear head by 3% NDCG or less, the linear weights describe the world and scalars may be quoted. Otherwise the world is non‑linear and you must report partial dependence curves instead of numbers. On this run the linear head wins by 0.8% — NDCG@10 of 0.772 against 0.766, on queries held out by query and never by row — so scalars are legitimate here. Nobody in this industry states that rule, which is why they all quote scalars for models that do not have any.

Five rungs

QuestionMethod
L0Is the number stable?Cluster bootstrap by query — ten results from one SERP are one observation, not ten — plus a permutation noise floor
L1Does it rank like Google?Group‑held‑out NDCG@10 against honest baselines
L2Does it travel?Unseen clusters and time windows; weight drift doubles as an update detector
L3Is it causal?Within‑query fixed effects, near‑twin matching, DiD across updates
L4Does it survive contact?Frozen‑model forward prediction, and split tests on client URLs

Governance rule: nothing reaches a client deck without a verdict, an interval, and a date it was last checked. No factor that has not cleared L3 or L4 may drive a recommendation. The keyword density result above is why that rule has to be written down rather than assumed — L0 through L3 can all be fooled by a badly measured instrument. Only L4 cannot, because in a split test you change the real thing rather than a proxy for it.

Three different things people call “importance”

Predictive

How much does knowing this help reproduce Google's order? What 0.84 usually means.

Causal

Change it on a page — how many positions? Needs matching, or better, an experiment.

Actionable

Effect size divided by cost to change. The only one that belongs in a client deck.

They routinely disagree by a factor of two. A tool that prints 0.84 without saying which it means is selling a horoscope.

Sizing the test before running it

The binding constraint is not day‑to‑day rank wobble, which averages away over a longer window. It is per‑URL drift between the two periods — a competitor moved, a link died, a page got re‑crawled — which averages out only over URLs. Measured, not assumed, at 28+28 days and 80% power:

§ 7

What is real and what is not

Being explicit, because the distinction is the whole point.

  • Real: every method and every statistic. The IRLS solver, the bootstrap, the IPW correction, the errors‑in‑variables adjustment, the matching, the DiD, the power analysis. All of it runs and all of it is reproducible with a seed.
  • Synthetic: the SERPs. Deliberately — a harness with known ground truth is the only place these methods can be audited rather than merely applied. The live wiring (DataForSEO, CrUX, Search Console → BigQuery) is in the repo and disabled behind an explicit flag, because it spends money and touches other people's servers.
  • Not measured at all: the effort scores behind the return‑per‑effort table. Those are a judgement about how much work one standard deviation costs, labelled as such everywhere they appear. Yours would be better than mine — you have the delivery data.

Conclusions hold across seeds. Runs at 7, 123 and 999 all produce an uncorrected pipeline that invents the decoy at 0.33–0.41 and loses the real factor, and a corrected pipeline that recovers it.
