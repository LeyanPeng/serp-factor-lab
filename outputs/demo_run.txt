  Look at what came top of that league table.
  A factor whose true weight is exactly zero ranks first on return
  per unit of effort -- because it is cheap to change and it is
  correlated with something that works. Fixed effects and matching
  both credit it: they are observational, so they inherit the
  measurement problem whole.
  This is the entire argument for the governance rule. L0 through L3
  can all be fooled by a badly measured instrument. Only L4 cannot,
  because in a split test you change the real thing rather than a
  proxy for it -- and nothing here reaches a client until it has.

    true -0.60   estimate -0.47   95% CI [-0.75, -0.19]   significant=True

  interval calibration over 400 replications: 94.5% coverage (nominal 95%)

 URLs per arm  min detectable (positions)
           30                       1.300
           60                       1.000
          120                       0.700
          200                       0.500
          300                       0.400

  The first test above failed to detect a real effect, and the table
  predicted it would. Working out the minimum detectable effect BEFORE
  running is what separates 'the change did nothing' from 'we could
  never have seen it'. Sizing a test off day-to-day wobble alone says
  thirty URLs is plenty. It is not: per-URL drift between the periods
  does not average out over days, only over URLs.

  client brand in this topic graph: absent from the topic graph
  That is the expensive finding. If the brand is not an entity in
  the topic, on-page work cannot fix it -- that is a schema.org,
  sameAs, Wikidata and citations problem, on a different budget.

  Why this sits in the same repo as the calibration harness:
  every other factor in the list produces a number an account
  manager reads. This one produces the same number AND the list of
  entities a writer has to add. The weight says how hard to push;
  the graph says where. Neither is much use without the other.
